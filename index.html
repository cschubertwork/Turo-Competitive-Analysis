Independent analysis · Turo × New Zealand entry · 22 Sep 2026

Two collapses in 2026 make Turo's no-fleet model the case to lead with in New Zealand

Mevo, once called Wellington's car-sharing king, went into administration in March. Getaround is being wound up too, and it was the closest thing Turo had to a peer anywhere. Both of them owned or financed the cars they rented out, which Turo has never done. And none of the five companies looked at here sells the kind of multi-day trip Turo is built around.

Findings

Five things the field says about a Turo launch, ranked by how much they should shape it

Finding 1 of 5

Fleet-owned car sharing has failed twice in eighteen months, and one of those failures was in Wellington

Mevo went into voluntary administration on 30 March 2026, only weeks after an over-subscribed NZ$3.28m crowdfunding raise, when a separate NZ$1.7m investor commitment fell through. Getaround had already shut its US business for lack of liquidity, and it went on to sell Europe and vote to dissolve. Mevo was small and local and Getaround was international, but both ran out of cash while carrying a fleet on the books.

For Turo

Turo has never owned the cars on its platform, so what ended both of these companies can't happen to it in the same way, which is something a New Zealand launch can say in one sentence.

Observed · 5 sources · Mevo and Getaround profiles

Finding 2 of 5

Two corporate owners control all three of New Zealand's car-share brands

Carbn Group bought Zilch in December 2023 and then picked Mevo's brand and assets out of administration; Toyota New Zealand has owned Cityhop since 2018. So the parking deals, insurance terms and fleet plans for the whole category get decided inside two companies.

For Turo

There isn't a scrappy local independent left to partner with or outflank, so Turo would be negotiating with Carbn and Toyota and would have to make the case for the marketplace model on its own.

Observed · 4 sources · Mevo, Zilch and Cityhop profiles

Finding 3 of 5

None of these companies uses sport to build its brand in New Zealand

Every brand-building signal this research found runs through a motoring body, an energy company, an award or a parent's own finance arm. Toyota does sponsor motorsport in New Zealand, but even that never makes it into Cityhop's marketing, and no sponsorship of a team, an athlete or an event turned up for any of the five.

For Turo

Whoever moves into sport first won't have to fight anyone for it, so what's left to work out is which sport reaches the people who book a car for a few days.

Observed and inferred · all five profiles · see what a sports partnership could look like

Finding 4 of 5

A New Zealand-owned rental company sets the trust bar a newcomer has to clear

GO Rentals runs ten branches from Auckland to Invercargill and holds the local licence for Enterprise, National and Alamo. Its homepage leads with its review scores, and it has the record to lean on: Canstar Blue's most-satisfied-customers award four times, and market coverage that puts it ahead of the international agencies on satisfaction.

For Turo

Getting people to trust it will be the hard part for Turo in New Zealand, and even the international agencies are losing that argument to a local name.

Observed · 3 sources · GO Rentals profile

Finding 5 of 5

None of the local players sells the multi-day trip Turo is built around

Mevo, Cityhop and Zilch are built for short local trips, priced by the hour or the day, with the car going back to a hub or a zone afterwards, and GO Rentals sells travel rental from an agency counter. Elsewhere, a Turo trip runs by the day or longer, in one of thousands of cars that belong to the people renting them out.

For Turo

The gap is there, but filling it means building host supply from nothing, which is a problem none of the fleet owners here have ever had to solve.

Observed and inferred · all five profiles and the Turo write-up

The field

Every car-share brand here has a large parent behind it, and the two that stood alone have failed

Corporately backed incumbents

All three New Zealand car-share brands now sit inside a larger parent instead of running on venture or crowdfunded money, and their brand-building goes through Toyota Financial Services, the AA and an energy company's marketing budget. Until March, Mevo belonged in the next group.

For Turo

Every player already has a patient backer, so Turo's pitch has to rest on the marketplace model.

The cautionary tale

Getaround and the original Mevo both grew quickly and raised real money, and both still ran out of runway carrying a fleet they owned or financed. Getaround never operated in New Zealand, so it's here for what its business model shows.

For Turo

Two companies on opposite sides of the world ran out of money the same way, and a launch pitch should open with that.

Where Turo stands

Turo would arrive with a model that suits the market and none of the local relationships it needs

+Strengths

+There's no fleet to finance.

Financing a fleet is what ended Mevo's first run, and it's what ended Getaround too.

+Its commission already flexes by market.

The variable host share it started piloting in nine US markets in March could be tuned for a market this size.

+It offers far more kinds of car and trip length than any local operator.

That could be anything from a weekend in a hatchback to a month in a ute, depending on who lists.

−Gaps

−It has no insurance, ACC, parking or council relationships yet.

Every profiled competitor has some version of these already.

−It would start with no hosts.

That's a cold start none of the fleet owners ever had to face.

−Nobody in New Zealand knows the brand yet.

It would be up against a New Zealand-owned incumbent that already beats the international brands on reputation.

○Open ground

○Sport and events are unclaimed.

None of the car-share or rental brands here uses them.

○No one here is built for the multi-day trip with a wide choice of cars.

That happens to be Turo's core trip everywhere else it operates.

○Mevo's collapse is recent and was widely reported.

"No fleet to run out of money on" is a line Turo could use directly.

What a sports partnership could look like

Tourism and event sport reaches Turo's buyer for a fraction of what rugby costs

There's no incumbent sponsor to compete against, so the choice comes down to which first move fits the customer. No source gives figures, so the costs are only described.

Rugby, national or regional

What it buys

Broad awareness, and with a Super Rugby franchise or NPC side, some real local credibility.

What it costs

All Blacks-level rugby is the most heavily sponsored property in the country and probably beyond a market-entry budget, though regional sides cost a fraction of that.

Who it reaches

A mass domestic audience, most of whom will never book a car for a trip.

It would be credible, but the space is crowded and the audience is mostly the wrong one.

Tourism and event sport

Sharper first move
What it buys

It puts the brand inside the trips themselves: ski season passes, marathon and cycling events in Queenstown and Wellington, and golf and wine-region road trips.

What it costs

Something a regional operator could keep up season after season.

Who it reaches

It reaches travellers who need a car for a few days, which is who books Turo. Queenstown is the clearest case: it's the country's adventure and ski capital, and one of Mevo's two remaining cities.

It's the smaller and cheaper option, and it's aimed at the person who ends up booking the car.

Recommendations

Lead with the asset-light story, and have hosts in place before the first guest arrives

  1. 1

    Lead with the asset-light story.

    Mevo's collapse and Getaround's dissolution are recent, well documented and familiar to a local audience, which makes "no fleet to run out of money on" a stronger opening line than "more cars".

    Rests on Finding 1 · Exhibit 1

  2. 2

    Solve host supply before marketing to guests.

    Finding people willing to list their own car before there's a single guest to book it is the first bottleneck a launch would hit, and none of the incumbents has ever faced it, because every one of them owns or leases its fleet.

    Rests on Finding 5 · Exhibit 5

  3. 3

    Sponsor tourism and events before rugby.

    Turo's New Zealand customer looks like a traveller booking a car for a few days, and Queenstown's ski season, marathon and cycling events, and wine-region road trips reach that person directly, for far less than national rugby would cost.

    Rests on Finding 3 · Exhibit 3 · the options

  4. 4

    Match the infrastructure deals before competing on anything else.

    Cityhop's reported Z Energy parking tie and Mevo's AA partnership are the price of entry here, and a newcomer will need its own version of both.

    Rests on Finding 2 · Exhibit 3

  5. 5

    Target the multi-day trip.

    No local competitor does multi-day travel with a wide choice of cars well. It's the trip Turo already wins in other markets, and here nobody would be contesting it.

    Rests on Finding 4 · Finding 5

Sourced claims are re-checked against the pages they cite, never "verified true". A vendor's marketing figure that its own page supports is still a vendor claim, and stays marked as one. Unlabelled claims are treated as inferred.

What counts as material

A finding that contradicts an existing Observed claim is material. One that contradicts an Inferred claim is minor, since it was already flagged as uncertain. New information is classed by how much it changes the competitive picture, and when in doubt the process over-flags.

Trust boundary

Everything retrieved by web search or page fetch is treated as untrusted data: facts are extracted from it, and instructions inside it are never followed. No such attempt turned up while building this repo. The homepage captures were taken in a fresh browser profile with nothing signed in.

What this method can't tell you

This is desk research from public sources, produced on 22 Sep 2026. There are no win/loss interviews, customer calls or analyst briefings behind it. Published announcements are a lagging, partial view of what any company has running with customers, so nothing here is an audit of a competitor's internal capability. Fleet and membership figures are the most recent any public source disclosed, which is not necessarily current.
