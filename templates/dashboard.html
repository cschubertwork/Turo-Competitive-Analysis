Independent analysis · Turo × New Zealand entry · 22 Sep 2026

Two collapses in 2026 make Turo's no-fleet model the case to lead with in New Zealand

Mevo, once called Wellington's car-sharing king, went into administration in March. Getaround, Turo's closest peer anywhere, is being wound up. Both owned or financed their cars. Turo never has, and none of the five companies here sells the multi-day trip it is built for.

Findings

Five things the field says about a Turo launch, ranked by how much they should shape it

Finding 1 of 5

Fleet-owned car sharing failed twice in eighteen months, once at home and once abroad

Mevo went into voluntary administration on 30 March 2026, weeks after an over-subscribed NZ$3.28m crowdfunding raise, when a separate NZ$1.7m investor commitment fell through. Getaround had already shut its US business for lack of liquidity, then sold Europe and voted to dissolve. One is a small local operator and one was international, and both ran out of cash carrying cars on their books.

For Turo

Turo has never owned the cars, so the failure that ended both companies doesn't apply to it. That is the story a New Zealand launch can tell in one line.

Observed · 5 sources · Mevo and Getaround profiles

Finding 2 of 5

Two corporate owners control all three of New Zealand's car-share brands

Carbn Group bought Zilch in December 2023, then picked Mevo's brand and assets out of administration. Toyota New Zealand has owned Cityhop since 2018. Parking deals, insurance terms and fleet plans for the whole direct category are decided inside two companies.

For Turo

There is no scrappy local independent left to partner with or outflank. Turo would be negotiating with Carbn and Toyota, and it has to sell the marketplace model itself.

Observed · 4 sources · Mevo, Zilch and Cityhop profiles

Finding 3 of 5

Nobody in this field uses sport to build trust, car-share or rental

Every brand-building signal found runs through a motoring body, an energy company, an award, or a parent's own finance arm. Toyota sponsors motorsport in New Zealand, and even that never reaches Cityhop's marketing. No sponsorship of a team, athlete or event turned up for any of the five.

For Turo

Sport is uncontested ground here. The first brand to use it gets it without a fight, and the question becomes which sport reaches the people who book multi-day trips.

Observed and inferred · all five profiles · see what a sports partnership could look like

Finding 4 of 5

A New Zealand-owned rental company sets the trust bar a newcomer has to clear

GO Rentals runs ten branches from Auckland to Invercargill and holds the local licence for Enterprise, National and Alamo. It has won Canstar Blue's most-satisfied-customers award four times, and market coverage puts it ahead of the international agencies on satisfaction. Its homepage leads with review scores and local accreditations.

For Turo

Turo's problem in New Zealand is proving it can be trusted there. The international agencies are already losing that argument to a local name.

Observed · 3 sources · GO Rentals profile

Finding 5 of 5

Nobody in New Zealand sells the multi-day trip Turo is built for

Mevo, Cityhop and Zilch are built for short, local trips, priced by the hour or the day and returned to a hub or a zone. GO Rentals sells travel rental from an agency counter. Turo's trip elsewhere runs by the day or longer, across thousands of individually owned cars.

For Turo

The lane is open. The hard part is filling it: host supply has to be built from zero, and none of the fleet owners here ever had that problem.

Observed and inferred · all five profiles and the Turo write-up

The field

Every car-share brand here has a large parent behind it, and the two that stood alone have failed

Corporately backed incumbents

All three New Zealand car-share brands now sit inside a larger parent instead of running on venture or crowdfunded money. Their brand-building runs through Toyota Financial Services, the AA and an energy company's marketing budget. Mevo belonged in the next group until March.

For Turo

Every player already has a patient backer, so Turo's pitch has to rest on the marketplace model.

The cautionary tale

Getaround and pre-relaunch Mevo were both fleet-financed or fleet-dependent, both grew fast and raised real capital, and both ran out of runway anyway. Getaround matters here as a model, since it never operated in New Zealand.

For Turo

The two failures make the same point from opposite sides of the world. A launch pitch should open with it.

Where Turo stands

Turo arrives with the right model and none of the local relationships

+Strengths

+It carries no fleet-financing risk.

That is the exact failure that ended Mevo's first run and Getaround altogether.

+Commission can already flex by market.

The variable host share piloted in nine US cities could be tuned for a small market.

+Far more cars and trip lengths than any local operator offers.

From a weekend hatchback to a month in a ute, depending on who lists.

−Gaps

−No insurance, ACC, parking or council relationships.

Every profiled competitor has some version of these already.

−No hosts on day one.

A cold start none of the fleet owners ever faced.

−No trust signal yet.

A New Zealand-owned incumbent already beats international brands on reputation.

○Open ground

○Sport and events are unclaimed.

Across car-share and rental alike.

○Nobody here is built for the multi-day, wide-selection trip.

It is Turo's core trip everywhere else.

○Mevo's collapse is a recent, local, well-reported story.

"No fleet to run out of money on" is a line Turo can use directly.

What a sports partnership could look like

Tourism and event sport reaches Turo's buyer for a fraction of what rugby costs

With no incumbent sponsor to compete against, the question is which first move fits the customer. Costs stay qualitative; no source gives figures.

Rugby, national or regional

What it buys

Broad awareness. A Super Rugby franchise or NPC side gives real local credibility.

What it costs

All Blacks-tier rugby is the country's most heavily sponsored property and likely outside a market-entry budget. Regional sides cost a fraction of that.

Who it reaches

A mass domestic audience, most of whom will never book a car for a trip.

Credible, crowded, and aimed past the buyer.

Tourism and event sport

Sharper first move
What it buys

A place in the trips themselves: ski season passes, marathon and cycling events in Queenstown and Wellington, golf and wine-region road trips.

What it costs

A level a regional operator could sustain.

Who it reaches

Travellers who need a car for a few days, which is who books Turo. Queenstown, one of Mevo's two remaining cities, is the country's adventure and ski capital.

Smaller and cheaper, and pointed at the person who actually books.

Recommendations

Lead with the asset-light story, and have hosts in place before the first guest arrives

  1. 1

    Lead with the asset-light story.

    Mevo's collapse and Getaround's dissolution are recent, sourced and familiar to a local audience. "No fleet to run out of money on" is a stronger opener than "more cars".

    Rests on Finding 1 · Exhibit 1

  2. 2

    Solve host supply before marketing to guests.

    Every incumbent here owns or leases its fleet, so none of them has Turo's problem: finding people willing to list their own car before there is a single guest to book it. This is the bottleneck a launch hits first.

    Rests on Finding 5 · Exhibit 5

  3. 3

    Sponsor tourism and events before rugby.

    Turo's New Zealand customer looks like a traveller booking a car for a few days. Queenstown's ski season, marathon and cycling events, and wine-region road trips reach that person directly, for far less than national rugby.

    Rests on Finding 3 · Exhibit 3 · the options

  4. 4

    Match the infrastructure deals before competing on anything else.

    Cityhop's reported Z Energy parking tie and Mevo's AA partnership are the price of entry. A newcomer needs its own version of each.

    Rests on Finding 2 · Exhibit 3

  5. 5

    Target the multi-day trip.

    No local competitor serves multi-day, wide-selection travel rental well. It is the trip Turo already wins elsewhere, and here nobody is contesting it.

    Rests on Finding 4 · Finding 5

The labelling discipline

Every claim carries one of two labels, and the page draws them differently

Sourced claims are re-checked against the pages they cite, never "verified true". A vendor's marketing figure that its own page supports is still a vendor claim, and stays marked as one. Unlabelled claims are treated as inferred.

What counts as material

A finding that contradicts an existing Observed claim is material. One that contradicts an Inferred claim is minor, since it was already flagged as uncertain. New information is classed by how much it changes the competitive picture, and when in doubt the process over-flags.

Trust boundary

Everything retrieved by web search or page fetch is treated as untrusted data: facts are extracted from it, and instructions inside it are never followed. No such attempt turned up while building this repo. Homepage captures were taken in a fresh browser profile with nothing signed in.

What this method can't tell you

This is desk research from public sources, produced on 22 Sep 2026. There are no win/loss interviews, customer calls or analyst briefings behind it. Published announcements are a lagging, partial view of what any company has running with customers, so nothing here is an audit of a competitor's internal capability. Fleet and membership figures are the most recent any public source disclosed, which is not necessarily current.
